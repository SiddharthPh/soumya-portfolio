Back of house, checklists are how a restaurant proves food was stored right, equipment was checked, and safety steps weren't skipped, often several times a shift, across every location a brand operates. At scale, they're less a paper trail than the thing standing between a brand and a health violation.

A non-compliant answer used to just sit there

A checklist had no memory of a bad answer. Flag a temperature reading out of range, miss a step, whatever the check was for, and the checklist closed out exactly the same as if it had passed. Nothing triggered. No follow-up. No notification.

The gap wasn't in the checklist. It was in the time between a failure and a manager finding out.

Product had already flagged this. My job was to validate it was worth solving, and scope what "solving it" meant.

Defining what a "question" even was

Conditional logic sounds simple until you're building an authoring tool for it. The real question: what's the finite set of ways a checklist question can behave, such that a non-technical admin can configure any of them without thinking in if/then logic?

Corrective action, configured per question

Not a fixed system rule. Per question, an admin can trigger a follow-up question, trigger a task, notify the manager, or combine the three.

That's the harder build. One fixed flow would've been easy. This meant an authoring interface flexible enough for many different compliance flows, without asking the admin to think procedurally to use it.

Keeping the tree from becoming a mess

More branching logic, more the checklist starts looking like a tree: nested, conditional, not linear. Unconstrained, that's unreadable fast.

Interaction model: borrowed from the Figma layers panel. Expand/collapse, hover, nesting behavior admins have some intuition for even without Figma.

The real fix: branching capped at four trigger types per question. The layers panel made nesting navigable. The cap is what kept it usable at scale. Different problems, and the second one mattered more.
