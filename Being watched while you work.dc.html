Being watched while you work

Figma's Observe mode lets a manager, or a teammate, follow your cursor and watch your file change in real time. It's built as a collaboration feature. I ran a small qualitative study to find out what it actually feels like on the other end of that.

RoleResearcher (solo): study design, interviews, thematic analysis, recommendations
ScopeDesign team members and design managers using Figma's Observe mode
TeamSolo project, TECH 697: Qualitative Research Methods, Purdue University
Timeline2023
ToolsZoom, Otter.ai, Dovetail, Figma
01 / CONTEXT

A feature for collaboration. Also a way to watch someone work.

Figma's Observe mode lets anyone in a file see, in real time, exactly what one collaborator is doing: every click, every drag. It's pitched as a way to work together. If a manager turns it on to check someone's report, it quietly becomes something else too: a live feed of unfinished work.

I wanted to know if that difference actually shows up for the people being watched, and what it costs them when it does.

02 / THE QUESTION

What I actually wanted to know.

There's already research on what constant oversight does to people at work: it wears down autonomy and trust, and it raises stress. Observe mode brings a version of that into a tool designers use all day long, so I went in with two questions.

QUESTION 1How does real-time visibility into a coworker's file affect communication, task allocation, and team cohesion?
QUESTION 2What does it do to individual productivity and emotional well-being, specifically around performance pressure, self-efficacy, and stress?

It seemed reasonable to assume that a manager using Observe mode could end up extending ordinary micromanagement into the digital workspace. That assumption is basically where this study starts.

03 / METHODOLOGY

Five interviews, two vantage points.

I ran semi-structured interviews with five people on design teams, based in the US and India, and split them into two groups with different questions for each.

Design managersHow and why they use Observe mode, and where they personally draw ethical lines around monitoring.
Design team membersHow being observed affects their focus, their work quality, and their relationship with the person watching.

Everything was recorded in Zoom, transcribed in Otter.ai, and coded in Dovetail. I moved from transcripts to codes to themes slowly, checking each pattern against the other interviews before I trusted it.

04 / WHAT I FOUND

It felt like micromanagement, not teamwork.

Discomfort was the most common reaction I heard. People called it intrusive, and more than one person used the word creepy. It wasn't just a passing feeling either: people told me they started second-guessing their own decisions in the moment, and spent more energy thinking about how their work looked than on the work itself.

"Creepy" Too intrusive Privacy concerns Self-doubt Vulnerable Anxious
05 / WHAT I FOUND

So people found ways around it instead of saying anything.

Nobody I spoke with brought the discomfort to their manager directly. Instead, they worked around it. Some worked at night, when fewer people were likely to be watching. Others did the real work in a private file and only moved it into the shared one once it looked finished. Different tactics, same goal: keep the messy part of the process away from an audience it wasn't made for.

“This is the reason why I work at night.” · A design team member, on choosing hours with fewer eyes on the file
“I use a separate file, but it's too much work.” · A design team member, on the cost of the workaround itself
06 / WHAT I FOUND

It wasn't all bad, though.

A few people genuinely liked it, in specific situations. It seemed to work well when it replaced something people would've had to schedule anyway, and it grated when it felt like watching for the sake of watching.

1A quick question, without booking a meeting. It became a way to have ad hoc Q&A on the spot.
2Tracking progress and project management, without asking anyone for a status update.
3A way for junior designers to learn, watching more senior decisions get made in real time.
07 / WHAT I FOUND

How people felt about it depended on a lot.

The same feature landed very differently depending on the situation. If people believed the monitoring was there to help, they were fine with it. If it felt like oversight for its own sake, they weren't.

What made it feel worse
Being watched on a personal, early-stage file, more than on one with other collaborators already in it. Observation paired with live audio. Being seen and heard at once, rather than just seen. A deadline closing in, which left less room to use the usual workarounds, right when the anxiety was worst.
What made it feel okay
Monitoring that read as support for productivity, rather than a check on the person. More people already present in the file, which spread the sense of being watched more thinly. Higher self-confidence going in, which tended to line up with more comfort being watched.
08 / THE REAL ISSUE

People didn't want less visibility. They wanted consent.

Almost nobody asked me to get rid of the feature. What came up again and again, from both groups, was some version of "just tell me first." A few people had already started asking verbally before turning Observe mode on, on their own, without anyone telling them to. That, more than anything else, told me what the product was actually missing.

09 / DESIGN RECOMMENDATIONS

What I'd change.

1Selective visibility. Let the person being observed choose what part of the file is visible, instead of showing the whole canvas by default.
2A notification when observation starts, so it's disclosed, not just possible.
3Clearer signals in the UI for who's in a file and how, so it isn't left to guesswork.
4Make it opt-in, not opt-out or on by default.
5A toggle between a collaborative mode and a private one, the same choice people were already making for themselves with their workarounds.
10 / REFLECTION

The ambiguity, it turns out, was the finding.

I expected people to feel anxious about being watched. That part wasn't surprising. What I didn't expect was how much the same feature, used the exact same way, could read as either helpful or invasive, depending only on whether there was consent and context around it. That's not really a policy problem. It's a design one: right now the product can't tell the difference between someone watching to help and someone watching to check up on you, and it leaves the person being watched to sort that out on their own.
